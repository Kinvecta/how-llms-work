Part four: where does the structure come from, and where does it break? The idea behind learned word vectors is sixty years older than the models. The linguist Zellig Harris proposed in nineteen fifty-four that words that occur in similar contexts tend to have similar meanings. It's called the distributional hypothesis. Vector models are that hypothesis made numerical. Words that keep showing up in the same company end up pointing the same way. A twenty ten survey by Turney and Pantel reports that the most popular way to compare two of these vectors is, exactly, the cosine. And when the word2vec authors list a word's nearest neighbors, they rank them by cosine too. So cat's neighbors are the words that kept appearing where cat appeared.

[8:22]
Then came the result everyone remembers. In twenty thirteen, Mikolov, Yih and Zweig took the vector for king, subtracted man, added woman, and found that the result lands very close to queen. Picture a parallelogram. The arrow from man to woman, slid over to start at king, points at queen. The method is simple: compute woman minus man plus king, then return whichever word's vector has the highest cosine with that point. It rests on one assumption. Every pair of words that shares a relationship, male to female, is separated by roughly the same offset. And remember: this is a two-dimensional cartoon of vectors that have hundreds of dimensions.

[9:04]
Now the fine print, because this result gets oversold. Even in the original paper, the model answered almost forty percent of eight thousand syntactic questions correctly. Impressive for twenty thirteen. Not magic. Later work went after the test itself. Tal Linzen argued in twenty sixteen that the cosine-based test mixes two things together: whether the offsets are really consistent, and which words happen to be neighbors of the target. And in twenty seventeen, Rogers, Drozd and Li argued that the offset method leans on plain vector similarity, so part of what looks like reasoning about relations is similarity doing the work. The parallelogram is a real pattern. It is not a law.

[9:45]
Which brings us to the myth: close vectors mean similar meaning. The case for it is everything we just saw. Similar contexts, similar vectors, cosine finds the neighbors. Here's the case against. Kawin Ethayarajh looked inside GPT-two in twenty nineteen and found that in its last layer, two random words have, on average, almost perfect cosine similarity. Random words.

[10:10]
In twenty twenty-one, Timkey and van Schijndel found out why. A small number of rogue dimensions, often just one to three out of seven hundred and sixty-eight, dominate the measure. A few huge coordinates swamp everything else, and cosine reads two unrelated words as nearly identical. So cosine similarity is a useful measuring stick, but in some models a few large coordinates can bend it. Close is a tendency. It is not a guarantee.

[10:39]
Let's put it back together. Words become vectors: ordered lists of numbers, read as points or arrows. The dot product multiplies matching entries and adds, and it measures alignment scaled by length. The norm is a vector's length, the square root of its dot product with itself. Cosine similarity divides the lengths out and keeps only direction. Similar contexts tend to produce similar vectors, which is where the analogy result comes from. And cosine is a proxy with known failure modes, so treat a high score as a lead, not a verdict.
