Part two: softmax. In episode three, a layer handed us a vector, h. The last layer of a language model produces one score for every token in the vocabulary. But scores are not probabilities. They can be negative, they can be huge, and they don't add up to anything in particular.

That step is softmax. Softmax of h, entry i, is e to the h i, divided by the sum over j of e to the h j. Exponentiate every score, then divide each one by the total. Three things follow straight from the formula. Every output is positive, because e to anything is positive. The outputs add up to one, because the denominator is the sum of the numerators. Now entropy. Entropy is the average surprisal, where each outcome is weighted by how often it happens. H of p equals minus the sum over i of p i times the natural log of p i. The log of a probability is never positive, so the minus sign makes H zero or more. Shannon called it a measure of choice and uncertainty, and wrote that only when we are certain of the outcome does H vanish. The more spread out the outcomes, the bigger H gets.

Part four: cross-entropy and perplexity. Entropy describes one distribution. But evaluation compares two: the target, q, which is what the text actually does, and the model, p, which is what it predicted. Cross-entropy, H of q and p, is minus the sum over i of q i times the natural log of p i. Surprise measured with the model's probabilities, averaged over what really happens. It splits into the target's own entropy, plus a mismatch term, the KL divergence, which is never negative and zero only when the model matches the target. So a model can tie the target's entropy, but never beat it.

By hand, one observed token. The true next word is mat. The target puts all its weight on mat, so the sum collapses to a single term: minus the natural log of zero point three zero. One point two zero four nats. Perplexity is e to the cross-entropy. e to the one point two zero four is three point three three, which is exactly one over zero point three zero. For a single observed token, perplexity is always one over its probability. If the word had been floor: one over zero point two zero, five. Sofa: six point six seven. The less the model expected the word, the more perplexed it is.

For a whole text of n tokens, average the surprisals of the tokens that actually appear. That average is the per-token cross-entropy. Perplexity is e to that average. Jurafsky and Martin write the same quantity in bits, as two to the entropy. Same number, as long as the log matches the base. Their intuition: perplexity is a weighted average branching factor.

So here's the myth: perplexity is the number of choices the model is picking from. It's partly right. For a uniform distribution it's exact: four equal outcomes, perplexity four. Otherwise, e to the entropy is only the number of equal choices that would give the same entropy. Three point eight zero isn't three point eight words. And on real text, perplexity is built from the observed tokens, not the model's own spread. Since cross-entropy is at least the target's entropy, perplexity is at least e to that, and equal only when the model matches.

Two more limits. First, confidence is not correctness. Take the confident model: sofa at zero point nine seven. Its entropy is a low zero point one seven nats. But if the next word is actually mat, at zero point zero one, its perplexity on that token is one over zero point zero one. One hundred. Very sure, and very wrong. Second, perplexities compare only across identical vocabularies. And episode one's taxi-map study is the same lesson at scale: near-perfect next-token predictions, with a map inside that doesn't match the streets. Perplexity says how surprised the model is by the text it sees. It does not say whether the model is right.

Let's put it together. The chain rule turns a sentence into a product of next-token probabilities, each conditioned on everything before it. Softmax turns scores into those probabilities: exponentiate, divide by the total, and only differences between scores matter. Entropy is the average surprisal of one distribution, in nats or bits. And perplexity is e to the average surprisal of the tokens the model actually saw.
