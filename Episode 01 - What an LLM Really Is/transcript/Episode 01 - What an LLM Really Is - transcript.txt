[5:36] Why prediction is difficult
At this point a reasonable person asks: predicting the next word? That's it? That sounds like autocomplete. It is autocomplete. The surprise is how much you have to know to do it well. In nineteen fifty-one, Claude Shannon measured how predictable English is by having people guess the next letter of a text. His estimate: with a long context, English carries somewhere between zero point six and one point three bits per letter, meaning roughly three quarters of the letters are, in principle, predictable from what came before. But to actually make those predictions, you need grammar, because "the cat sat on" is followed by a noun phrase. You need facts, because "the capital of Texas is" has one good continuation. You need to track a story, because "it" in our sentence means the cat. And you need to know that a question is usually followed by an answer. Prediction is the goal. Everything else is what the goal forces the model to learn.

[6:35] How the dials get set
How do the dials get set? Three stages, and we'll spend a whole part of the series on each. Stage one is pretraining. Take an enormous pile of text, and at every position, ask the model to predict the next token. The text supplies its own answers, so nobody has to label anything. The model's guesses start out random, and over weeks of compute the dials turn until the guesses are good. That's episodes six through fourteen, plus twenty-three.

[7:05] Post-training
Stage two is post-training. A pretrained model continues text; it doesn't follow instructions. The twenty twenty-two InstructGPT paper from OpenAI set the template for fixing that. Hire about forty people to write good answers to about thirteen thousand prompts, and fine-tune on those. Then show the model's answers to those same people, have them rank between four and nine candidates per prompt, and train a second model to predict the rankings. Then let the first model practice against the second one's score, on about thirty-one thousand more prompts. That's episode twenty-six.

[7:41] Reasoning and inference
Newer models add a third stage: reinforcement learning on problems with checkable answers, like math and code. DeepSeek's R one paper reported that, with this stage alone, their model's score on a competition math benchmark went from about sixteen percent to seventy-one percent. Treat that as a twenty twenty- five development, not a universal recipe. And then inference, which is the stage you use. Running the finished dials, one token at a time. Every stage before it exists to make those tokens good.

[8:14] Three claims checked
Three things people say about these models. For each one I'll give you the strongest evidence on both sides, because none of them is settled. Myth one: it's a database that copies text. What's true: the model does reproduce some training text word for word. Memorization is real, and episode twenty-nine is about it. What's wrong: there's no corpus in there to look up. A hundred and forty gigabytes of numbers cannot store the internet. Training compressed patterns into dials, and sometimes a pattern reproduces exactly what it came from. Remember "mat" at rank thirty-one. If our sentence were stored somewhere, it would have come back.

[8:57] Othello: an internal board
Myth two: it understands nothing. Here's the evidence that something is going on inside. Researchers at Harvard, MIT and Northeastern trained a small GPT on transcripts of Othello games, just lists of moves, no rules, no board. Then they looked inside. From the model's internal activations, a probe could reconstruct the current board with one point seven percent error, versus about twenty-six percent for an untrained network. The model built a board it was never shown. A follow-up found that if you label each square "mine" or "yours" instead of black or white, the board is encoded so simply that you can edit it with vector arithmetic and the model's next move changes to match.

[9:41] The taxi map counterexample
And here's the evidence for the other side. A team at Harvard, MIT and Cornell trained transformers on turn-by-turn taxi directions in Manhattan. Next-turn prediction was essentially perfect. But when they reconstructed the street map the models had implicitly learned, it bore little resemblance to Manhattan, and adding small random detours made performance collapse. So: models can build real internal structure from text alone. And strong prediction does not guarantee a coherent model of the world.

[10:13] Fluency and understanding
Myth three: it thinks like a person. The best-known case against this is the twenty twenty-one paper that coined the phrase "stochastic parrot." Its authors describe a language model as a system for haphazardly stitching together sequences of linguistic forms it has observed in its training data, according to probabilistic information about how they combine, but without any reference to meaning. That's one serious position. The Othello result is a serious reply. Fluency is not thinking, and whether these systems understand anything is a question researchers still argue about.
